How to mend clothes: simple sewing repairs that last

Most clothing repairs take ten minutes and a few basic stitches. Learning five of them keeps good garments in use and out of the bin, and a sewing machine makes several of them quicker still.

By the UK SEW editorial team · 21 November 2026 · 3 min read

Photo: Darling Arias on Unsplash

On this page

  1. Why mending is worth learning
  2. How to sew on a button
  3. How to fix a split seam
  4. How to darn a hole or patch worn knees

Why mending is worth learning

UK charity WRAP has found that extending the average life of clothes by just nine months would save £5 billion in resources. You do not need a statistic to see the point: a coat with a loose lining or a favourite shirt missing a button is usually one small repair from being wearable again.

Start with a small kit: a few hand needles, strong thread in common colours, small sharp scissors, pins and a thimble or seam ripper. Our haberdashery and accessories page covers more of the basics.

How to sew on a button

  1. Mark where the old button sat, and match the thread to the fabric or button.
  2. Use doubled thread with a knot at the end and stitch up through the fabric and a hole in the button.
  3. Lay a matchstick or pin on top of the button so you leave slack, and stitch through each pair of holes five or six times.
  4. Remove the spacer, wind the thread around the stitches under the button to make a short shank, and fasten off on the back.

The shank gives the button room to sit over the buttonhole instead of pulling flat.

How to fix a split seam

Turn the garment inside out and pin the split edges back together along the original stitch line. By hand, use a small backstitch for strength, overlapping the old stitching by about a centimetre at each end. By machine, sew a straight stitch along the line, reverse for a few stitches at the start and end, and trim loose threads. Test on a scrap first, and make sure the needle is sharp and suits the fabric: see our sewing machine needles guide. If you need a refresher on setting up, read how to thread a sewing machine.

How to darn a hole or patch worn knees

For a small hole in a knitted jumper or sock, darning rebuilds the missing threads: stitch rows of parallel running stitches across the hole, extending a little into sound fabric on all sides, then weave a second set of rows at right angles over and under the first. Use a darning mushroom or a jar to hold the fabric taut.

For worn trousers or elbows, an iron-on or sewn patch on the inside is quicker. Trim any frayed edges, cut the patch larger than the damage and sew around its edges. Patches also work well on denim and workwear, where a visible repair is part of the look.

Shorten a hem, then decide if it needs a machine

To take up a hem, measure and pin the new length, press the fold, and stitch with a slip stitch by hand (nearly invisible from the right side) or a straight stitch on the machine. Jersey and knit fabrics need a stretch needle and stitch; see sewing with jersey knits.

If you are mending regularly, a basic sewing machine pays for itself in time, and when yours skips stitches or jams, sewing machine repairs explains your options. A zip replacement or leather work may need a heavier machine or a professional, and it is fine to say so.

Frequently asked questions

What thread should I use to mend clothes?
Use a good-quality all-purpose polyester or cotton-wrapped thread that matches the fabric colour. For sewing buttons on coats or jeans, a stronger thread is worth using.
Can I mend clothes without a sewing machine?
Yes. Buttons, small seam splits, darns, patches and hems can all be done by hand with a needle and thread, and hand repairs are often the neatest option for delicate fabrics.
When is a repair not worth doing?
If the fabric has worn thin all over, a repair may not hold for long. Zips, leather and structured tailoring are often better left to a specialist repairer or alteration service.
