How to choose a sewing machine

Too many stitches, not enough guidance? Here is a calm, practical way to choose a sewing machine that suits what you actually sew.

By the UK SEW editorial team · 14 October 2026 · 3 min read

Step 1: list what you will sew

Write down your three most likely projects. Mending and alterations, dressmaking with woven cottons, stretchy jersey, quilting, bags and upholstery all ask different things of a machine. The heaviest fabric on your list sets the minimum standard.

Buy for the heaviest fabric you sew, not the lightest.

Step 2: pick a machine type

  • Mechanical: simple, durable and easy to repair. Ideal for beginners.
  • Computerised: more stitches, automatic buttonholes, needle up/down and speed control.
  • Heavy-duty: stronger motor for denim and canvas.
  • Overlocker: a second machine for neat seams on knits, not a replacement.

Compare them on our sewing machines page.

Step 3: features that genuinely help

  • A reliable one-step buttonhole.
  • Adjustable stitch length and width.
  • A free arm for sleeves and trouser hems.
  • Presser foot pressure control.
  • Standard 130/705H needles and easy-to-find bobbins.

Step 4: decide new or used

A good used machine that has been serviced and tested by a specialist is often better made than a budget new one. Our used machine checklist explains what to look for.

Questions to ask in the shop

  • Which needle system and bobbin class does it use?
  • Is there a hard cover or carry case?
  • What comes in the box: feet, bobbins, needles, a manual?
  • Who services it locally, and how long are parts available?
  • Can I bring it back if it doesn’t suit?

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying hundreds of decorative stitches you won’t use instead of a better-built machine.
  • Choosing a toy-grade mini machine for real projects.
  • Forgetting the cost of a service on a cheap second-hand machine.
  • Not checking the machine handles several layers of your heaviest fabric.

Mechanical vs computerised in more detail

A mechanical machine sets stitch length and width with dials and uses cams and gears to form stitches. It has fewer things to go wrong, tolerates a knock, and any good engineer can service it. The trade-off is fewer stitches, a simpler buttonhole and no speed limiter.

A computerised machine uses a circuit board and stepper motors. You gain push-button stitch selection, memorised settings, a needle that stops up or down, speed control (helpful for beginners and children) and often an automatic needle threader. Repairs to electronics can cost more, so buy from a brand and dealer with good support.

Think about the bobbin system

Drop-in (top-loading) bobbins sit under a clear cover, so you can see how much thread is left and they are easy to change. Front-loading bobbins sit in a removable metal case, which many experienced sewers prefer for fine tension control. Neither is better for everyone, but try both in the shop.

Accessories worth having from day one

  • Zipper foot and buttonhole foot (usually included).
  • Walking foot if you plan to quilt or sew knits and checks.
  • Spare bobbins of the correct class.
  • A selection of needles: universal 80/12 and 90/14, ballpoint and jeans.
  • A hard cover or carry case if you will take it to classes.

Step 5: test-sew before you buy

Take your own fabric to a dealer and try straight stitch, zigzag and a buttonhole. In Leicester, PK Sewing Machines has sold new and used machines since 1982, close to the streets where Leicester’s hosiery trade once made stockings for the world.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on a first sewing machine?
Enough to get a solid machine from a supported brand with a good buttonhole. A serviced used machine from a specialist is a good way to stretch your budget.
Is a computerised sewing machine better for beginners?
Not necessarily. A simple computerised machine with speed control helps some beginners, while others prefer the simplicity of a mechanical machine. Build quality and an easy buttonhole matter more.
Should I buy an overlocker at the same time?
Most people start with a sewing machine and add an overlocker later, once they sew jersey or want faster seam finishing.
